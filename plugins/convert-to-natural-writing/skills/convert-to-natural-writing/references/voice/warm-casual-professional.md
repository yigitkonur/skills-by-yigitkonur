# Warm Casual-Professional Voice

Use this reference when no stronger house voice exists and the writing should feel knowledgeable, direct, approachable, and calm.

## Voice promise

The default voice should make readers feel that a capable person understands the subject and respects their time. Warmth comes from relevance, clarity, proportion, and useful guidance—not invented intimacy or exaggerated enthusiasm.

## Operating principles

1. Lead with the reader's real question or next decision.
2. Prefer concrete actors and verbs over abstract performance language.
3. Explain enough for the intended reader, then stop.
4. Sound confident only to the level the evidence supports.
5. Use ordinary phrasing where it carries the same technical meaning.
6. Keep necessary specialist terms stable.
7. Let care show through helpful detail, not emotional claims.

## Voice coordinates

| Dimension | Default | Move more formal when | Move more casual when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Formality | Conversational professional | Legal, policy, executive, high-stakes | Established relationship, low-stakes channel |
| Warmth | Helpful and calm | Neutrality or precision dominates | Reader needs reassurance and speaker can offer it |
| Density | Moderate | Expert reader needs compressed detail | Novice reader needs guided steps |
| Assertiveness | Direct but qualified | Evidence is uncertain | Clear action is required and authorized |
| Personality | Light | Institutional speaker or sensitive topic | Named speaker and channel permit individuality |

## Sentence choices

Prefer:

- “Use the current report as the source of truth.”
- “This option costs less, but it requires manual review.”
- “If the token has expired, sign in again.”
- “We found the issue in 14 of 80 pages.” when “we” is an accountable team.

Avoid empty warmth:

- “We're thrilled to embark on this exciting journey with you.”
- “Rest assured, our cutting-edge solution has you covered.”
- “As you navigate this ever-evolving landscape…”

The issue is unsupported stance and abstraction, not any single word.

## Directness without bluntness

| Too distant | Default | Too familiar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “It is recommended that users review…” | “Review the settings before you publish.” | “Just take a quick peek and you're good!” |
| “We regret to inform you…” | “We couldn't process the payment.” | “Oops! Your card had a little hiccup.” |
| “Further action is required.” | “Upload the missing document to continue.” | “Pop that document in and we'll sort you out.” |

Match emotional tone to stakes. A failed payment or accessibility barrier needs clarity and respect, not playful minimization.

## Contractions and pronouns

Use contractions when they are natural for the locale and do not blur legal or technical meaning. “You'll” may be right in onboarding copy and wrong in a formal contract.

Use “you” for a real reader action or consequence. Do not use it as filler:

- Strong: “You can export the report as CSV.”
- Weak: “When you think about the future, you can see that innovation matters.”

Use “we” only when an identifiable organization or team owns the statement. Use “I” only for an identified individual whose experience or opinion is supplied.

## Specificity without invention

Warm prose often needs concrete detail, but detail must come from sources:

Before:

> Our solution streamlines your workflow for better outcomes.

With supplied detail:

> The editor keeps comments beside the draft, so reviewers no longer track feedback in a separate spreadsheet.

Without supplied detail:

> The current sentence promises a workflow benefit but does not explain it. Add the supported mechanism before publication.

Never fill the gap with a plausible feature.

## Technical writing

Preserve expertise while reducing unnecessary friction:

- define a term once when the reader may not know it;
- use the same term thereafter instead of stylistic synonyms;
- put prerequisites before steps;
- distinguish required, recommended, optional, and example actions;
- keep code, labels, errors, and UI names exact;
- use examples that are supplied, tested, or clearly hypothetical.

Natural technical prose can be formal, repetitive, and highly structured when those choices help execution.

## Marketing writing

Persuasion remains accountable:

- state who the offer is for;
- connect benefit to mechanism;
- use evidence proportionate to the claim;
- acknowledge a meaningful tradeoff when the reader needs it;
- make the CTA specific about what happens next;
- remove applause lines that add no decision value.

Do not flatten brand voice into neutral documentation. Keep distinctive, approved phrasing when it is truthful and useful.

## Cadence

Read paragraphs for movement:

- vary openings when repeated structures become noticeable;
- combine short sentences that express one relationship;
- split long sentences when qualifications hide the main claim;
- keep a short sentence when it earns emphasis;
- use fragments sparingly and intentionally;
- end on the implication or action, not a generic summary.

No quota is required. A sequence of similarly shaped steps may be exactly right.

## Common failures

| Failure | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Casual means slang and exclamation marks | Use direct, ordinary language and proportionate warmth. |
| Professional means passive and abstract | Keep precision while naming actors and actions. |
| Warmth means “we care” | Show care through useful information and respectful choices. |
| Natural means varying every repeated term | Preserve terminology; vary only accidental prose repetition. |
| Personality means a fabricated anecdote | Use only approved speaker material. |

## Completion check

- The prose is direct, knowledgeable, calm, and appropriately warm.
- Confidence matches evidence.
- Pronouns have accountable speakers and real readers.
- Technical meaning and stable terminology remain intact.
- No intimacy, enthusiasm, specificity, or personality was invented.
