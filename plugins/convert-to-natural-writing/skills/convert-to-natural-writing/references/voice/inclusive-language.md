# Inclusive Language

Use this reference when content describes people, communities, identity, disability, age, gender, race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, or socioeconomic status.

## Accuracy before euphemism

Inclusive editing is not a synonym-replacement exercise. It requires accurate, relevant, specific, and context-aware description while respecting how people identify themselves.

## Core decisions

For each identity reference, ask:

1. Is the characteristic relevant to the point?
2. Is the label accurate and sufficiently specific?
3. Is self-identification or community guidance available?
4. Does the sentence reduce a person to one characteristic?
5. Does grammar assign agency, blame, deficiency, or normality unfairly?
6. Does the locale use different accepted terms or identity conventions?

If identity is irrelevant, omit it. If it is analytically necessary, name it precisely enough to support the claim.

## Source authority

Rank terminology sources:

1. The person or community's supplied self-identification.
2. Current approved organizational or domain guidance.
3. Target-locale community and professional usage.
4. General style guidance such as APA bias-free language.
5. Editor preference.

Preferences differ within communities. Do not universalize one label or silently overwrite an individual's chosen wording.

## Person-first and identity-first language

Neither is universally correct:

- Person-first: “person with a disability”
- Identity-first: “disabled person,” “Autistic person,” depending on preference and context

Follow the subject's preference or relevant community guidance. When neither is available, use the accepted standard for the target locale and domain, and flag high-stakes uncertainty.

## Precision and relevance

Weak:

> Vulnerable people struggle to use digital services.

Better when supported:

> People using screen readers could not reach the payment button because it had no accessible name.

The revision names the affected users and barrier. It avoids turning vulnerability into an inherent personal trait.

Weak:

> The elderly may find the interface confusing.

Better when age is supported and relevant:

> In usability sessions with adults aged 70 to 82, five participants missed the low-contrast link.

Do not invent the study or age range; this example is valid only when those facts are supplied.

## Agency and framing

Prefer language that locates barriers and actions accurately:

| Problematic framing | Better principle |
| --- | --- |
| Person “suffers from” a condition by default | Use neutral description unless suffering is the sourced point. |
| Users “fail” to complete inaccessible flow | Name the interface barrier and observed outcome. |
| A community is “hard to reach” | State which channel or practice failed to reach them. |
| One group is “normal” | Name the actual comparison group. |
| Identity used as metaphor | Describe the actual product or behavior. |

Preserve agency when people act. Use passive voice deliberately when the actor is unknown or protecting a person's privacy—not to hide institutional responsibility.

## Group comparisons

When comparing groups:

- define the comparison population and time period;
- avoid implying biological or causal explanations without evidence;
- distinguish observed association from cause;
- state relevant uncertainty and sample limitations;
- avoid presenting one group as the default human baseline;
- check whether aggregated labels hide meaningful differences.

The protected-content ledger must include comparison sets and causal force.

## Multilingual considerations

Identity language does not transfer mechanically. Grammatical gender, honorifics, collective nouns, reclaimed terms, and legal categories differ by language and region.

- Do not import an English-language identity label without confirming local use.
- Preserve diacritics and names as supplied.
- Ask or flag when pronouns or forms of address materially affect accuracy.
- Avoid forced neutral constructions that become ungrammatical or obscure meaning in the target language.
- Use fluent community-aware review for sensitive or public-facing content.

## Quotations and historical language

Do not silently rewrite a direct quotation. If a quotation contains outdated or harmful language:

- confirm that the quotation is necessary;
- preserve accuracy;
- provide context when the publication requires it;
- consider paraphrase with attribution if exact wording is not essential and authorization exists;
- follow archival, academic, or legal quotation rules.

## Common failures

| Failure | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Replacing one label everywhere without context | Follow self-identification, locale, and domain guidance. |
| Adding identity details for specificity | Use only supplied, relevant details. |
| Removing identity from analysis where it matters | Preserve relevant, evidence-backed distinctions. |
| Treating inclusive language as euphemism | Name people, barriers, and comparisons accurately. |
| Applying English conventions globally | Seek target-locale community guidance. |

## Review escalation

Require accountable review when:

- the subject's preference is unknown and wording is prominent;
- a label is contested or changing;
- the content affects rights, access, healthcare, safety, or policy;
- group comparisons could imply blame or causality;
- translation crosses different identity frameworks;
- the editor lacks relevant community or locale competence.

## Completion check

- Identity references are relevant, accurate, and no broader than evidence.
- Self-identification and current locale guidance outrank editor taste.
- Barriers and institutional actions are not displaced onto people.
- Comparisons retain scope, uncertainty, and causal discipline.
- Sensitive unresolved choices have an accountable reviewer.
